# Circuit City — Research Dossier

*Version 2. Excerpt check: 41 of 41 claims verified against their sources; the rest are listed under Open Questions.*

## Summary
Founded in 1949 as the Wards Company, Circuit City pioneered the electronics superstore format in the 1970s, reached $12.43 billion in net sales in fiscal 2007 and developed CarMax, which it spun off to shareholders in 2002. It filed for Chapter 11 on November 10, 2008, about 19 months after laying off about 3,400 better-paid associates, and closed its last stores on March 8, 2009. Our read (interpretation): a series of decisions — the 2000 appliance exit, the 2003 rejection of CompUSA's offer, the 2007 layoffs — weakened it against Best Buy, a rival it reportedly could have bought for $30 million in 1988.

## The Twist
In late 1988, Circuit City had an opportunity to buy Best Buy, then a growing competitor, for $30 million; CEO Richard Sharp rejected the offer. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_City)

## Story Beats
- **Rise:** Samuel Wurtzel founds the Wards Company in 1949; his son Alan becomes CEO in 1972. Renamed Circuit City Stores in 1984, it pioneered the electronics superstore format in the 1970s. In 1991 it developed the CarMax used-car superstore concept, which it spun off to shareholders in October 2002.
- **Peak:** Fiscal 2007 (ended February 28, 2007) is the sales high in the cited filings: $12.43 billion in net sales, 654 domestic stores (642 Superstores and 12 others) and about 43,000 domestic employees (FY2007 10-K). The chain kept expanding after that: 682 Superstores at February 29, 2008, and 712 Superstores plus 9 outlets at October 31, 2008.
- **Cracks:** In July 2000 it left the large-appliance business. In 2003 it rejected a $1.5 billion buyout offer from CompUSA. On March 28, 2007, it laid off about 3,400 better-paid associates, to re-staff the positions at lower market-based pay. Fiscal 2008 net sales fell to $11.74 billion, with a net loss of $319.9 million. Our read (interpretation): together these decisions weakened the company as Best Buy grew.
- **Fall / comeback:** CEO Philip Schoonover resigned on September 22, 2008. Blockbuster, which had bid $1 billion to $1.3 billion in April 2008, withdrew in July. On November 4, 2008, Circuit City announced 155 store closings; on November 10 it filed for Chapter 11. In January 2009 it signed an agency agreement to liquidate 567 stores.
- **Aftermath:** The last stores closed on March 8, 2009, and Systemax bought the brand name, trademarks and website for $14 million. In January 2016 it was reported that Ronny Shmoel would revive the brand; Circuit City Corporation relaunched online on August 22, 2018. CarMax, the concept Circuit City developed in 1991, had been spun off to shareholders in 2002.

## Hook Ideas
1. In 1988, Circuit City reportedly had the chance to buy Best Buy for $30 million, and said no.
2. Circuit City laid off about 3,400 of its better-paid associates in March 2007. About 19 months later, it filed for bankruptcy.
3. Circuit City invented CarMax, then spun it off to its shareholders in 2002.
4. It kept expanding while failing: 682 Superstores in February 2008, 712 by October, then 155 closings and a bankruptcy filing within days.
5. Our read: Circuit City is a case study in mistaking cost-cutting for strategy.

## Best Angle for a Short
The 1988 decision: Circuit City reportedly had the chance to buy Best Buy for $30 million and said no (Wikipedia, citing Alan Wurtzel's 2012 book). Our read (interpretation, not a sourced finding): Best Buy became the competitor Circuit City could not outlast.

## Best Angle for Long-form
A 60-year rise and fall, 1949 to 2009: from the Wards Company to $12.43 billion in sales, CarMax, the 2007 layoffs and liquidation. Our read (interpretation): a story about a company that kept expanding while its fundamentals weakened.
